Good morning, everyone. Today we are looking at mitochondrial DNA, and why it behaves so differently from the DNA in the nucleus of your cells.

Let's start with the mitochondrion itself. You will remember it as the powerhouse of the cell. It is where aerobic respiration makes most of the cell's ATP.

What is less well known is that mitochondria carry their own genome. It is a small, circular molecule, about sixteen and a half thousand base pairs long.

That tiny genome codes for thirty seven genes. Thirteen of them make proteins for oxidative phosphorylation, and the rest make the transfer and ribosomal RNA the mitochondrion needs to build those proteins.

Why would an organelle have its own DNA at all? The best explanation is the endosymbiotic theory. Around two billion years ago, an ancestral cell engulfed a free living bacterium, and instead of digesting it, the two came to depend on each other.

Over time, most of that bacterium's genes moved into the host nucleus. Mitochondrial DNA is what is left behind, which is why it still looks so much like a bacterial chromosome.

There is also a lot of it. A single cell can hold hundreds or even thousands of copies of mitochondrial DNA, compared with just two copies of each nuclear chromosome.

Now, here is the part people find tricky, so let me try to explain maternal inheritance. You inherit your mitochondria from your mother, but not because your father has none. His sperm do have mitochondria, in the midpiece, and some of them do get into the egg. But they are tagged and destroyed, mostly, so what you end up with is your mother's mitochondrial DNA, which she got from her mother, and not her father's, even though her father's mother passed hers down the same way. And if some of the egg's own mitochondria are different from each other, which we call heteroplasmy, then which ones you get is partly down to chance, so two siblings can inherit the same mother's mitochondria and still not have exactly the same mix.

If that felt like a lot, don't worry. We will come back to it with family tree diagrams on Thursday.

Because mitochondrial DNA comes down the maternal line and does not recombine, it changes only through mutation. Those mutations build up at a fairly steady rate.

That steady rate makes mitochondrial DNA a useful molecular clock. By comparing sequences between people, researchers can estimate how long ago two maternal lineages shared a common ancestor.

This is the idea behind the so called Mitochondrial Eve, the most recent woman from whom every living human inherits their mitochondrial DNA. She was not the only woman alive at the time, just the one whose maternal line never broke.

Mitochondrial DNA also matters in medicine. Mutations in it can cause mitochondrial diseases, which tend to hit tissues that need a lot of energy, like muscle, the brain and the heart.

Because of maternal inheritance, an affected mother can pass a mitochondrial disease to all of her children, while an affected father almost never passes it on.

Forensic scientists use mitochondrial DNA too. There are so many copies in each cell that it often survives in old bones, hair and teeth, when nuclear DNA has already broken down.

So, to sum up. Mitochondria have their own small, circular genome, left over from an ancient bacterium. It comes down the maternal line, it changes only by mutation, and that makes it useful for tracing ancestry, diagnosing disease and solving crimes.
